# Chapter 1: The Illusion of the Indie Lottery & The Algorithmic Shift

> *"The industry told independent musicians they were being given a global stage. What they actually built was a digital casino where the house takes 99.7% of the chips and blames the player when the wheel stops spinning."*  
> — Ron Higgins ("The Keeper", Mandan, ND)

---

### 1.1 The Democratization Myth: The Open Door with the Iron Wall

In the mid-2010s, a golden promise swept through the global music community. 

For the first time in human history, independent artists were told that the iron grip of the major record labels had been permanently broken. You no longer needed an A&R executive in New York, a radio plugger in Los Angeles, or a manufacturing contract in Nashville to share your recorded music with the world. Digital distributors—ReverbNation, DistroKid, TuneCore, CD Baby, and later BandLab—promised direct, universal access. Whether advertised at twenty to forty dollars a year for barebones uploaders, or billed at **$19.95 every single month ($239.40 per year)** for comprehensive artist distribution packages like ReverbNation Pro, any songwriter with an audio interface and a laptop was told they could have their tracks placed on the exact same digital shelf as the biggest pop stars on Earth.

Your song would sit on Spotify, Apple Music, Deezer, Tidal, and Amazon Music, right next to the Beatles, Taylor Swift, and Pink Floyd. The world was your audience. You were told that talent, authenticity, and perseverance were all that stood between you and a sustainable independent career.

It was an intoxicating vision. And for millions of artists, including Ron Higgins in Mandan, North Dakota, it felt like the dawn of a democratic renaissance.

Between 2018 and 2025, Ron released eight master-quality original rock singles under the artist name **CletusMaxx** through ReverbNation Distribution, published under his own entities—**CletusMaxx Music Publishing (BMI)** and **Ron Higgins Music**:
1. *Fights on Life* (February 9, 2018)
2. *I Don't Give A Damn Anymore* (March 22, 2018)
3. *Midnight* (May 14, 2018)
4. *The Lesson* (August 21, 2018)
5. *In the Back of Your Mind* (April 9, 2019)
6. *To Much Truth in Honesty* (April 27, 2019)
7. *Liddle Redd Rooster* (April 22, 2022)
8. *Play* (April 29, 2025)

These were deeply personal tracks that Ron had written, arranged, recorded, engineered, produced, and performed the vast majority of parts on (singing all lead and backing vocal harmonies, tracking the majority of guitars, and playing bass, synthesizers, and percussion) in his own home studio in Mandan, North Dakota. Select guitar tracks across the catalog featured supplemental guitar work performed by longtime musical friend **Greg Metzger**—an authentic musical friendship where Greg was gifted and also purchased gear from Ron over those years to build his own home recording setup, while Ron recorded, engineered, and mixed several of Greg's own original songs in his Mandan studio, released under Greg's own name.

The rhythm backbone of the recordings carried its own poignant story of brotherhood and practical studio craft. The drummer and close friend in CletusMaxx, **Darin Geiger**, never tracked on any of the commercial releases. This was not because he wasn't good enough—Darin was an incredible, powerhouse drummer—but because Ron made the deliberate executive production decision to program all drum tracks digitally. In a solo apartment home studio, precision drum programming fit Ron's solitary writing and recording workflow far better, eliminating the agonizing days of drum kit mic'ing, room acoustic treatment, and phase alignment. 

Even though Darin did not track on the releases, the friendship was steadfast. Ron encouraged him constantly, even gifting him a complete computer recording system capable of digital drum programming and DAW editing so Darin could expand his natural drumming gifts into modern production. But Darin was an honest, self-employed craftsman who operated a small engine repair shop in Mandan, working with his hands all day, and he preferred not to spend his evenings staring at a computer screen learning complex audio software. Tragically, Darin passed away on November 22, 2021, during the COVID pandemic. Though he never played on the released masters, his spirit, laughter, and groove remain forever woven into the history of CletusMaxx.

*(For listeners who encounter archival photos on the CletusMaxx YouTube channel or legacy ReverbNation pages showing Darin behind a drum kit in an apartment: those photographs captured Darin playing a quiet electronic drum kit that Ron kept in his apartment—perfect for apartment volume control—taken during sessions when Ron was producing and recording Greg Metzger's original songs. Other sessions occurred in Greg's basement home studio, documented on Ron's earlier music website before the domain lapsed. But for the eight official CletusMaxx master singles, precision digital drum programming remained Ron's dedicated solitary studio method.)*

The production was pure human craftsmanship: real tube amps, hand-played Fender and Gibson guitars, driving basslines, nuanced vocal takes, and thoughtful analog-modeled mixing wrestled into shape over hundreds of late-night studio hours on the North Dakota prairie. This was not disposable loop-assembly or ghost-produced pop; it was authentic, blood-and-sweat rock and roll.

And the global numbers followed. Across those eight tracks, the catalog racked up **1,550,515 logged streams**. On paper, CletusMaxx had achieved what 95% of indie musicians dream of: over 1.5 million people had listened to his music.

### 1.2 The "Seeding" Era: Escaping the Zero-Plays Ghost Town

To understand how indie music actually works—rather than the romanticized fairy tales peddled by music business gurus—one must confront the cold psychology of the "Zero-Plays" stigma.

In the late 2010s, platforms like SoundCloud, ReverbNation, and Spotify were flooded with hundreds of thousands of new songs every week. If an unknown artist from Mandan, North Dakota uploaded an original track and it sat at `Plays: 0`, almost no human being on Earth would click on it. Human psychology in digital environments is conditioned by social proof: listeners assume that a track with zero plays is amateurish, poorly recorded, or unworthy of their attention.

In that era (2017–2020), the open-platform music world operated under a tacit understanding: **you had to prime the pump.**

Independent promotional packages were openly marketed by distributors and platform affiliates. You could spend twenty or thirty dollars to purchase an initial "1,000 plays" package on ReverbNation or SoundCloud. Everyone knew the initial 1,000 plays were promotional seeds. They weren't a vanity flex; they were the digital equivalent of dropping a dollar bill into your own guitar case before opening your mouth to busk on a street corner. It signaled to the passerby: *someone has paused here before.*

Furthermore, as a legitimate, paying subscriber, Ron did what thousands of dedicated independent creators across the globe quietly did to survive: he created personal playlists of his catalog and kept them playing on a continuous loop around the clock on his own computers in Mandan.

For years—between 2019 and 2023—this continuous looping maintained a steady pulse in the machine. It generated roughly **$100 to $175 per month** in distribution earnings, which seemed to justify the effort and the ongoing $19.95 monthly ReverbNation Pro subscription. The elevated play counts provided social proof, which caught the algorithmic radio waves and drew in secondary listeners who added the tracks to their personal playlists.

In 2024, Ron sat at his console and looked at the cold, hard numbers. 

Keeping computers running 24 hours a day, 365 days a year was burning electricity, running up bandwidth, wearing out components, and requiring endless daily babysitting. On top of that, ReverbNation was charging its recurring $19.95 monthly Pro distribution fee. When Ron calculated the true cost of operation, the truth was undeniable:  
> *"I was spending more money and energy keeping the loop going than the platforms were actually paying me."*

So in 2024, Ron made the conscious, sober choice to pull the plug. He stopped the continuous loops entirely and simply let the catalog sit on its own natural momentum.

What happened next was an immediate, unvarnished revelation:  
Within months, the numbers piddled down to almost nothing. In 2025, the entire 8-track catalog generated just **$20.01 for the entire year**. In 2026, it generated **$2.37**. There were occasional small blips in fan curiosity, but without the continuous artificial engine revving the machine, the corporate streaming ocean simply swallowed the catalog whole.

### 1.3 The Algorithmic Shift: The Clampdown on the Commons

Beginning around 2022 and accelerating aggressively through 2024 and 2026, the streaming ecosystem underwent a violent corporate consolidation. Facing pressure from Wall Street investors demanding higher profit margins and facing saturation from 120,000 new tracks uploaded every day, the major streaming platforms pivoted:

1. **The 1,000-Stream Cutoff Threshold:**  
   Spotify instituted a policy stating that tracks receiving fewer than 1,000 streams in a 12-month period would receive **zero royalty payouts**. The fractional pennies generated by those songs would not be returned to the listener or the artist; instead, they were pooled into a central fund and redistributed to the top 0.1% of global superstars.
2. **Automated "Artificial Streaming" Fines:**  
   Platforms deployed crude, proprietary algorithmic filters to detect "abnormal listening spikes." If a third-party scam playlist scraped an indie song without the artist’s knowledge—a predatory practice where bot farms add thousands of indie tracks to arbitrary playlists to mask their illicit activity—the streaming platform did not investigate the bot farm. Instead, they struck the innocent artist!  
   Distributors like DistroKid and ReverbNation began passing down **$10 penalty fines per track** directly to indie musicians, threatening permanent termination of their accounts and catalog deletion.
3. **The Squeezed Downstream Hose:**  
   As corporate distributors like BandLab/ReverbNation reorganized their business models, they began aggressively monetizing their own creators. They erected paywalls around basic artist profiles, demanded annual subscription renewals to keep back-catalog tracks live in digital stores, and simultaneously saw their reported streaming payouts dry up to fractions of a cent.

The artist was caught in an impossible vice:
* Pay an ongoing annual rent ($19.99 to $49.99 per year) just to keep your songs on the shelf.
* Receive an average payout of **$0.003 to $0.0035 per stream** (meaning you need roughly 3,000 streams just to break even on a single song's annual distributor fee).
* Live under perpetual threat of an automated algorithmic strike, with zero access to a human customer service agent to plead your case.

### 1.4 The Asymmetry of Honesty: The One-Way Algorithmic Mirror

What makes this modern streaming trap truly poisonous to the creative spirit is its fundamental **moral asymmetry**.

Consider the double standard:
* **The Indie Creator's Burden:** The human songwriter must verify their legal identity, register their tax identification number (EIN/SSN), submit W-9 or W-8BEN forms, provide verified bank accounts, assign lossless audio masters, verify ISRC codes, affirm under penalty of perjury that they own 100% of the composition and recording, and live under the terrifying threat of automated bot strikes. Honesty, compliance, and procedural perfection are ruthlessly demanded of the artist.
* **The Corporate Platform's Opacity:** The streaming platform and the digital distributor operate behind a wall of total secrecy. They do not reveal the exact formula of their "streamshare" royalty pools. They do not explain how automated bot strikes are determined. They do not disclose their back-room deals with major labels. And in many documented cases, platforms have quietly populated their own ambient and relaxation playlists with "ghost artists"—tracks commissioned at flat rates with no royalty obligations—to siphon money away from independent artists.

### 1.5 The Honest Surrender: The Beach Boys, the 120,000-Song Ocean, and Waking Up

Why did the music, on its own, fail to sustain natural momentum once the loop was retired?

This is the question that keeps millions of independent musicians awake at night, drowning in self-doubt. The natural human tendency is to blame the art: *"Maybe the songs weren't good enough. Maybe the mix wasn't punchy enough. Maybe I'm just not talented."*

Ron Higgins—with sixty-three years of songwriting behind him—saw through that lie. 

The deeper truth is that **the music alone, in isolation, cannot generate the unnatural inertia required to break through a saturated corporate world.** 

Consider the history of popular music:
* In 1961, when Brian Wilson and **The Beach Boys** caught their first big break, they famously called into a radio station and disguised their voices to win an initial contest, generating the spark that caught Capitol Records' attention. But what happened next? **Capitol Records deployed the massive corporate architecture.** They hired national radio pluggers to lobby DJs across America; they paid for distribution trucks to place vinyl records in thousands of department stores; they financed national concert tours, print magazine features, and television appearances. It took a multi-million-dollar corporate army to turn great music into a national phenomenon.
* Today, that corporate backing does not exist for independent bedroom and apartment artists. Instead, **over 120,000 new songs are uploaded to streaming platforms every single day**—more than 43 million tracks a year dumped into an indifferent digital ocean.
* The few modern "indies" who build sustainable audiences do so through an exhausting physical grind: twenty-something musicians living in a beat-up van, playing 180 sweaty dive-bar gigs a year, hawking $35 t-shirts and hats at a merchandise table after every show, and spending four hours a day acting as social media influencers begging TikTok algorithms for views.

For an elder songwriter in his seventies, crafting melodic, heartfelt rock in a solo apartment studio in Mandan, North Dakota, **that road is neither possible nor desirable.** Ron was never going to pack into a tour van, grind 200 bars, or dance on video apps. 

And when Ron recognized that his music alone could not overcome an ocean of 120,000 daily uploads without either a giant corporate record label or an exhausting 24/7 loop rig, **he threw in the proverbial towel.**

Throwing in the towel was not an admission of defeat. **It was the moment of waking up.**

It meant refusing to play an un-winnable lottery. It meant refusing to be an unpaid machine operator for Spotify while paying monthly rent to ReverbNation. It was the conscious decision to step off the commercial treadmill, collect his masters, and choose sovereign freedom.

The system was never broken. It was functioning exactly as it was designed: to extract the life energy, catalog assets, and subscription dollars of millions of hopeful independent musicians to support a multi-billion-dollar corporate infrastructure.

The only way to win a rigged game is to stop playing.
