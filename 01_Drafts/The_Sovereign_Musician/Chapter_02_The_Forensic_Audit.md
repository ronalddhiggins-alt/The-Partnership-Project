### 2.2 The "Squeezed Hose" Discrepancy: Aggregator vs. Statutory Collections

The most important forensic discovery of our audit occurred when we cross-referenced ReverbNation’s distribution reports against Ron’s **direct statutory collection registrations**.

Every commercial song generates three completely distinct revenue streams:
1. **Master Recording Sound Recording Royalties:** Paid by the digital streaming platform (DSP) to the distributor/aggregator (ReverbNation/BandLab), who passes it down to the artist after taking their cut.
2. **Public Performance Royalties (Composition):** Generated when a song is broadcast, streamed, or performed publicly. Collected directly by Performing Rights Organizations (**BMI** or **ASCAP**) and paid 50% to the Songwriter and 50% to the Music Publisher.
3. **Mechanical Royalties (Composition Reproduction):** Generated every time a composition is digitally reproduced or streamed. Established under statutory law by the U.S. Congress (the Music Modernization Act) and collected directly by **The MLC (Mechanical Licensing Collective)**.

Between 2023 and 2026, a bizarre discrepancy emerged:

While ReverbNation was reporting that Ron’s catalog was yielding near-zero trickles—sending statements for $0.18, $1.12, or $0.00 while simultaneously prompting Ron to renew his paid subscriptions—Ron's direct statutory portals told an entirely different story.

**This is the Squeezed Hose in action.**

When an indie creator relies on a commercial distributor as their sole interface with the world, the distributor controls the spigot. If the distributor alters their payment thresholds, implements processing fees, changes their accounting intervals, or reorganizes their corporate priorities, the artist's payout is choked off at the source.

Meanwhile, the statutory bodies—which operate under federal mandates and do not take a commercial commission from the songwriter—were faithfully logging the exact same streams, matching the metadata, and paying out real money directly to Ron’s bank account!

This single realization shattered the illusion: **You do not need an aggregator to validate your worth as a songwriter. If your metadata is clean, statutory law protects your royalties without requiring you to pay an annual subscription tribute to a corporate middleman.**

### 2.3 The Honest Hobbyist: Scrupulous Taxes and IRS Schedule 1

One of the great unspoken secrets of the indie music community is tax compliance. Many artists operate under the table, treating small streaming payouts as "pocket cash" or attempting to claim massive commercial business deductions on their home studios to wipe out their personal income tax liabilities.

Ron Higgins took the opposite approach: **The Honest Hobbyist.**

Under United States tax law (IRS Code Section 183), an activity is classified as a *business* if it is operated with an ongoing expectation of commercial profit, typically demonstrated by earning a profit in at least three out of five consecutive years. If an activity does not meet that threshold, it is legally classified as a **Hobby**.

Because Ron was not touring, was not selling corporate merchandise, and was not operating an active commercial enterprise, he never attempted to hide behind fictitious corporate write-offs. Instead:
* Every single dollar received from ReverbNation ($7,698.61), BMI, and The MLC was scrupulously documented.
* All royalty earnings were reported annually on **IRS Form 1040, Schedule 1, Line 8z ("Other Income — Music Royalties / Hobby Income")**.
* North Dakota state income tax was paid in full on every penny earned.

### 2.4 The Economic Absurdity: Paying Rent on Fractional Pennies

Let us conclude this forensic audit with the cold, unvarnished mathematics that every indie artist must confront:

Suppose you have an 8-song catalog on a modern digital distributor.

#### Model A: The Full Artist Suite (e.g., ReverbNation Pro)
* **Monthly Subscription:** **$19.95 per month** ($239.40 per year).
* **Eight-Year Investment (2018–2026):** **$1,915.20 in cash fees** paid directly to the platform just to keep the profile active and the tracks live in digital stores.
* **The Reality of Back Catalog:** Once the initial promotional push ends, back-catalog streaming payouts often trickle down to pennies—or zero under platform thresholds. Paying **$20 every single month ($240 a year)** to receive $0.18 or $1.12 from streaming stores is an annual cash drain of over $230 out of your own wallet!

#### Model B: The Barebones Annual Aggregator (e.g., DistroKid / TuneCore)
* **Annual Distributor Cost:** $19.99/year (basic) to $49.99/year (multi-artist or premium legacy tiers). Over eight years, that represents between **$160 and $400 in direct fees**.
* **The Payout Rate:** At $0.003 per stream, if your 8 songs generate a combined 5,000 streams across an entire year, your gross streaming income is **$15.00**.
* **The Net Economic Return:** You paid $19.99 to earn $15.00. **You lost $4.99 for the privilege of letting Spotify host your music.**

Under both models, if your catalog receives fewer than 1,000 streams per track, your payout under modern streaming policies is **$0.00**. Whether paying $20 a year or $20 a month, you are paying rent on a storage locker that the landlord has padlocked from the inside.

When an artist stays on this treadmill, it isn't because the math makes sense; it is because of **fear and ego**:
* Fear that if the music isn't on Spotify, it "doesn't exist."
* Ego that demands being able to tell friends at a party, *"You can find me on Spotify."*
